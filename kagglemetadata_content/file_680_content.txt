Why "crash landing"? Crash landing is landing nonetheless- not smooth, something that you would like to avoid, but better than nose-diving into the land.

It is an unplanned event, but sometimes it is the better alternative than no alternative.

Sometimes, you need to initiate such an [emergency landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_landing) as a nudge, to generate an incentive to change.

Example: an Italian politician while in office during the oil crisis of the early 1970s had one lamppost out of two switched off, to signal the need for energy savings.

Decades later, he admitted that the move did not save any money- actually generated costs, but was to signal to structurally individualist Italians the need to save energy, as otherwise each and every one of them would consider that saving energy would be something that they as individuals could excused from.

In Italy, the need to more or less dramatic "nudge" to generate incentives to change is a routine- and since I was a kid frankly always saw this kind of paternalistic "we know better" approach to administration and interaction with citizens- then, found funny how routinely politicians complained about lack of interest for the "commons" by citizens.

Leadership should also educate and leave behind a better place than they found, not just float around to avoid displeasing anybody- and, in tribal Italy, any choice is going to displease somebody.

Developing a shared long-term interest is more complex than using instantaneous "nudges" to motivate.

I am boring- one of those people who, even as a consultant, after a meeting, unless there is a banner stating not to do so, after everybody leaves a meeting room switches off the lights.

Or that, when finds an open faucet with running water, again unless there is a writing stating not to touch it, closes the faucet.

And other similar activities.

The concept? energy, water, space are in my view a "common"- which is more than just a shared resource- is a resource that we have been temporarily assigned, but whose use should be sensible to cover not just for our generation, but also for future generations.

Notably because, often, at least since WWII, we leave behind for future generations the maintenance costs of badly designed and cheaply developed quickly delivered infrastructure built for our own purposes, not for a long-term concept.

And not just in Italy or the European Union.

As for the concept of "common"- what I wrote above should be, in my view, extended to what makes the overall market economy that we selected to use as our system to work properly.

Since the beginning of this century, on the natural resources, financial architecture of our society, and physical infrastructure (to name just a few elements) we have been routinely confronted with the side-effects of our collective short-term approach- and we are starting to reap, all across different domains, the consequences.

Be it the financial crisis of two decade ago, or the realization due to a couple of tsunamis around a decade ago of how much our world is interlocked and with no resilience if a single point of failure fails, to say nothing about the 2020-2021 COVID crisis and its side-effects.

We have been restructuring and fixing using a tinkering approach ("least resistance") for a couple of decades.

So far, with really razor-thin successes: as even the recovery and resilience facility, those 750bln EUR set in motion by the European Union during the COVID crisis, frankly focused more on "recovery" than "resilience" (and investment), and the "NextGenerationEU" was more a paper tiger and good for events and leaflets, than a real choice in prioritization of funding allocation.

<b>Conclusions and next steps</b>

In the early 1990s, one of my selling point for cultural and organizational change first, then methodologies, and then again change but evidence-based (i.e. integrating data in decision-making, as did from the late 1980s for customers), was that change should be considered a continuum, not a crisis-to-crisis activity.

Something that implies building a special type of capabilities: an attitude to change as a stepping stone, not an achievement.

Something that, incidentally, would convert any initiative in that direction, successful or not, into a learning lesson.

Instead of the usual scapegoating exercise at the end, with its companion identification of somebody to collect the pieces prepared by the scapegoat, and raise from the assembled debris as a winner.

As shared in the past, in project management, when intervening to recover activities, sometimes when working as facilitator or account manager I had to cheer up the actual project manager.

So, I explained the overall social dynamics of many projects where the initial business case and scope definition let's say that is a bit too superficial.

The first project manager is the one that actually tries to make it work- and, in the process, fixes the business case, scope, and gets the blame for the results.

The second project manager, inheriting expectations but at least vague understanding of the issues, is called in to solve- and, first, has to hit the ground running, assess, and pave the way to actually bring it back on a route that deliver completion.

Still, in many cases, what the second project manager reports is not necessarily something that management wants to hear, so a third project manager is involved.

This time, there third project manager inherits:
_ what the first encountered as roadblocks and preparedness issues
_ what the second assessed and identified as potential fixes (and risks to manage)
_ most important, if the project or initiative is still floating, a willingness from management to have some sort of conclusion.

Including by finding somebody to take the blame for all the changes and mistakes across, allowing the third one to complete into a success.

Of course, there would often be quite a change between the initial that was assigned to the the first project manager and what is assigned to the third project manager.

Anyway, generally the third one is able to swiftly deliver courtesy of what the second did, and claim a success.

Personally, often I have been called to be the second and continuing as the third- and, in some cases, of course the second: just the role of the third is usually assigned to somebody who is coopted into fast-track, hence has to receive a support network and ready-made successes.

If you ever worked in large consulting companies, it happens actually that, depending on business politics needs, either on your side or the customers' side, you can be asked to play one of the three roles- and a different one in different initiatives.

Two elements change significantly since the late 1990s, in my experience:
_ technological change accelerated and expanded the amount of information, not necessarily its quality
_ in most cases, the managerial capabilities are still developed not just as was pre-Internet, but even before computing was available on each desk (i.e. PCs).

Add to that volatility and easier access to even more data in shorter timeframes, and you build up uncertainties.

The second point in that list above becomes then even more critical than in the past: if you train and prepare your managers for their ability to navigate corporate politics, not for delivery with a systemic perspective, you generate incentives for tunnel vision- and spending so much time and resources hedging, that decision-making becomes at best convoluted.

And anything but timely, and certainly not pre-emptive.

Meaning: the rotation between first-second-third might be detrimental (albeit I keep see it still used), as each "handover" has a phase-in and phase-out.

Not for the project manager (that could be reduced to zero- and I had cases where frankly barely met the previous project manager), but for the time, that has to adjust to different styles of communication, and different operational cultures.

So, in our times, probably it is better to have just one that covers the three phases- notably because too many "agile" initiatives and projects frankly actually are "superficial" or a bit "hurried up": as they say about using AI, better to think before you start prompting, as otherwise you get dragged into rounds upon rounds of something that, if you had spent some time thinking, you would have simply avoided starting at all.

Or, at least, would have been subject to iterations and increments not of the product or service or benefit delivered, but of the original intent, well before you started even building a roadmap.

Couple old approaches and new times, and you get so often what Orwell called in "1984" and described in the appendix as ["newspeak"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspeak), that you barely ever notice it.

Some roadmaps are so superficial, that could frankly be generated by the dozen from your favorite AI in a single day.

Then, trying building the needed capabilities to deliver, a mere exercise of capacity planning, and to deliver even just a first couple of iterations of the first increment that you want to be the result, and you will see how easy is obfuscating your way into a rabbit hole.
