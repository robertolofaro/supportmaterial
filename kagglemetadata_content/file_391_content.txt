Recently (September 2019) I posted two articles on the theme "assessing systemically": on the [business side](index.php?page=374) and the [political side](index.php?page=375).<br /><br />Anyway, no assessment can be carried out without an appropriate amount of data.<br /><br />Moreover, within the context of European Union integration, there must be a certain amount of at least harmonization of data used to chart progress.<br /><br /><b>Assessing systemically: what "data" does imply</b><br /><br />If you want to assess a system, you need perspective- i.e. need also to observe it from different viewpoints, and possibly from outside the system itself (or using an external framework of reference).<br /><br />As for the title, I am referring specifically to [open data](index.php?page=380) (the link outlines the concept and talks about an example).<br /><br />In business, harmonization usually works where there is either a leading party, or a third-party that is acknowledged to be superpartes<br /><br />About the latter: e.g. after a M&amp;A between equals- frankly, I have doubt that <strong>any</strong> merger will ever be between equals- eventually, in my experience and observations also from others I worked with, eventually the balance becomes unbalanced.<br /><br />So, let's say anyway that harmonization, one way or the other, is needed.<br /><br />To play it safe, if you want to harmonize European Union toward further integration, a reality check is using both data that are from within the EU as designed by EU, and data from EU that is "harmonized" toward something else.<br /><br />Hence, my use of open data from both EU sources (e.g. Eurostat or ECB) and UN sources.<br /><br />I do apply the same with Italy, my birthplace: I use both data from the national statistics office, other local and national sources, and Eurostat.<br /><br />What are open data? I will let you read [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_data) for a collection of definitions.<br /><br />In my case, I consider mainly data from local and national authorities and international institutions that enable individual and corporate citizens to see what is going on, to keep the pulse.<br /><br />Furthermore, I consider open data something that has a "business as usual" element- in business as well as with national statistics, the old "lies, damned lies, statistics" is always in the back of your mind whenever you cannot access raw data.<br /><br />So, a continuously updated stream, possibly right after the fact or passing through layers that aren't within the same chain-of-command is a way to avoid that data are "morphed" out of recognition.<br /><br />In business, there are other consequences on a time-delay on collection, processing, distribution of data.<br /><br />I will not tell share specific examples (where, how, when) over the last 30+ years, but e.g. sometimes instead of having monthly data collected at the end of the month, to be then processed one-two weeks later, and then transferred to a third-party that needs to take action on them (e.g. a customer paying an invoice produced based on those data), maybe it is better to find an agreement to have and deliver incomplete data before the end of the month, and then forward the resulting document to the third-party that will act on it.<br /><br />In some cases, you will reach an agreement to e.g. have interim processing and have as complete as you can data by, say, end of month minus two days, process on end of month minus one, and then deliver on the same day the resulting information.<br /><br />What would happen to what is related to the last day or two? Depends- sometimes, you add a forecast, and then fix up in the following time period; sometimes, it is more acceptable simply to "shift" that data in the following period.<br /><br />Of course, it depends on your domain- see as an example the annual routine on public accounts from European Member States.<br /><br /><b>Assessing systemically: seeing the whole picture</b><br /><br />Assessing coordination activities over the last few decades, notably in Italy.<br /><br /><b>Thinking about organizational data filtering</b><br /><br />I called it [DataDemocracy](index.php?page=392), but it is more about "data politics" and "data transparency" than elective (i.e. the "democracy" part).<br /><br />My concept is simple: do not expect "polished" applications: as I did explain above, my attitude developed first in politics, then in the Army, then in business on Decision Support Systems and then in negotiations and coordination of (services, management, software) delivery activities is probably Draconian.<br /><br />I rather have data earlier in a way that it is understandable and allows to spot trends and patterns, than wait for that perfect looking Powerpoint or Keynote presentation that is delivered either one week later or filtering out most of the information according to criteria based upon the past.<br /><br />Then, if it becomes useful, you can "industrialize", as we say in Italian, i.e. turn into a "business as usual".<br /><br />But, as I said in business in few languages since the early 1990s, while delivering change activities, beware of those scheduled meetings that are always the same, present always the same information, within the same format, at the same time frequency.<br /><br />That is the "ritual/informational"- not the "decision support" part of information dissemination.<br /><br />Useful, but one should avoid mistaking the former for the latter.<br /><br />Data democracy has more to do with what will be the future integration of data in our everyday life.<br /><br />Actually, I should rephrase that: the "transparent integration of data"- as data are already everywhere, but we still lack, more than the legal, the mental and conceptual framework needed to all benefit from that.<br /><br />My point is simply that it doesn't matter to declare "transparency" and pushing data online, unless those data are structured and accessible for those who will need to act on them, without any further "interpretation layer" that is actually a selective element.<br /><br />I saw that already decades ago in practice: sometimes, more data did not imply data-driven decisions, as the assumptions adopted by the layers between the raw data and the final consumer (e.g. senior manager), to reduce data to a quantity that would fit existing presentation tools available to said consumer (from management reports to the first visible layer of an Executive Information System or dashboard or collection of Key Performance Indicators)...<br /><br />...often distorted information.<br /><br />Our brain isn't really able to "see" large volumes of data presented as numbers- yet, if you have a huge amount of data, some people can spot the discrepancies or trends- discrepancies or trends that would almost certainly become "blurred" (the side-effect of consolidation, summary, etc) by the traditional tools associated with converting into an indicator.<br /><br />Most tools on the business intelligence market are good at making information "homogeneous", i.e. cover the "ritual/informational" part, but often the datasets delivered get layer upon layer (I remember Executive Information Systems with... hundreds of screens).<br /><br />Until, eventually, an assistant or junior aide is given the task to have a look and summarize.<br /><br />So everybody ends up happily covering a role: the data producers, the data processors, the data steward, the data aide, and the decision-makers receiving highly-digested data.<br /><br />Then, when something happens, it is a matter of backtracking to find why it wasn't seen before: just read the 9/11 report, or the quote from a high USA official on lack of visibility of information, or watch the documentary interviewing Robert McNamara on ["The Fog of War"](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0317910?ref_=nv_sr_srsg_0).<br /><br />The latter contains interesting segments organization (small or large, national or multinational, multi-industry or in a narrow domain, doesn't really matter).<br /><br />To a certain degree, harmonization into a Key Performance Indicator (KPI) by necessity "blurs" structural differences.<br /><br />It isn't necessarily true for each and every KPI- but I will give you just a couple of examples between the 7 KPIs that I extracted from the SDG database (UN and Eurostat).<br /><br /><b>The meaning of data: two SDG cases</b><br /><br />As posted within an article posted on 2019-11-26 [just few doubts on #national and #local challenges <i>in conferences and on the media, often SDGs are presented as if they were something of interest for developing countries.<br /><br />A significant distortion: the model represented by the SDGs really is worth looking at also in our developed countries- it is a matter of global convergence toward a social and governance model, like it or not.<br /><br />The paradox is that those working on SDGs, including national statistical bureaux and multinational corporations, know fairly well that SDGs apply to both countries and companies: it is only the media and general (and, unfortunately, most of the business) public that seem to consider SDGs as a kind of "feel good initiative"</i><br /><br />Now, within the SDG dataset that I extracted from the sources there are 342 SeriesCode, with 37 different "Units" (i.e. units of measure).<br /><br />Anyway, while most KPIs are quite simple to understand, i.e. the higher the value, the better you are, or viceversa, some instead are split across different categories in different years or countries, as not all the EU countries reported the same information, with the same level of details, in each country.<br /><br />So, I selected just 7 "SeriesCode" to compare EU 27 Member States on (yes, I extracted but then removed the data for UK- as it is confirmed that on 2020-01-31 Brexit will be official, no point), with just 3 units of measure.<br /><br />Reason? I wanted to check on few elements concerning my focus of interest, not everything for everybody.<br /><br />Then, I will share online from January 2020.<br /><br />Whenever reading data and statistical tables, it is worth remembering to use your brain.<br /><br />There are few books around (many can be read for free on Internet), but, in order to explain the concept about "understanding data" and "understanding context" while doing an assessments before making decisions, I will share a couple of examples.<br /><br />As those that worked with me know, if I have to express something negative I prefer to use myself as an example of error you could make- and how to avoid them.<br /><br /><b>Example 1: what you seem to see is what you get</b><br /><br />The first one is quite short: if there are no other variables involved, or their impact might be assumed to be irrelevant, and the data are expressed as e.g. a percentage toward the achievement of a goal, you might well do a straight comparison.<br /><br />If you read "IT_USE_ii99 Internet users per 100 inhabitants", probably it is easy to understand what it means: it is a percentage, the more people use Internet, the better, if you are talking about creating a data-centric economy.<br /><br />Look at this table: self-explanatory, it seems.<br /> <img src="https://robertolofaro.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/20191230_it_use_ii99_0.png" /><br /><br />Now, the details explain the same story:<br /> <img src="https://robertolofaro.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/20191230_it_use_ii99_1.png" /><br /><br />Of course, we are in reality ignoring few elements, such as:
<ol>
<li>is the average income mix the same (as this might impact on access to technology)?</li>
<li>is the average educational level the same (ditto)?</li>
<li>are the access costs comparable (but this in Europe should be part of the convergence e.g. on roaming)</li>
</ol>

<br /><br />Now, what happens when instead you are completely out-of-the-line if you ignore those "background variables"?<br /><br /><b>Example 2: there is a story behind the numbers</b><br /><br />If you read: "NV_IND_SSIS Proportion of small-scale industries in total industry value added (%)", what does it mean?<br /><br />This is an example of what you can get, by using averages and difference from averages: <img src="https://robertolofaro.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/20191230_nv_ind_ssis_0.png" /><br /><br />Now, you should consider a couple of questions:
<ol>
<li>does the EU have the same mix of small-scale industries all across Member States?</li>
<li>does Italy economic structure match the "average EU"?</li>
</ol>

<br /><br />Keeping those questions in mind, have a look at what the "average of values" returns:<br /> <img
